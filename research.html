Cultural Evolutionary Psychology

People’s beliefs and perceptions can be fundamentally reshaped by their historical and cultural context. This assumption draws from constructionist theories of psychology, in which socially transmitted conceptual knowledge is embedded in basic psychological processes like feeling emotion and judging right from wrong. We draw from cultural evolutionary models to formalize how this process of social transmission works. The lab’s work has contributed to the emerging field of “historical psychology,” which uses formal theory and computational methods to understand how the mind has changed over time.

Scale, Threat, and Strong Social Norms

One branch of the lab’s work focuses on how people adapt to living in “societies of strangers,” cultural groups where size and mobility increase the rate of anonymous interactions. In a number of projects, we show how humans develop stronger social norms in these societies, especially when they face ecological threats that increase the value of coordination. We consistently find that larger-scale societies have stronger social norms. We believe this happens because people grow more concerned with norm violations as societies grow, and when large-scale societies face new threats like conflict or natural disaster. Our experiments have found that, when facing these concerns, people are more likely to support political executives who can enforce law and order, and they are more likely to ascribe punitive and controlling qualities to deities. We also show that strong social norms may indeed be adaptive for promoting coordination and cooperation when large-scale societies face threats, as evidenced through cross-cultural variation in case and death rates in the COVID-19 pandemic. Recent work on this topic emphasizes the role of institutions like government, religion, ritual, and kinship in managing norm enforcement and cooperation in large-scale societies.

Social Cognition in Societies of Strangers

Our lab also studies how living in “societies of strangers” can reshape people’s psychological concepts, promoting the reliance on abstract, decontextualized concepts like “morality,” which guide our social inferences. People today use these concepts when they speak about how their neighbor is “extraverted” or their friend is often “angry,” but these kinds of categories are often vast oversimplifications of social behavior. We have shown that these kinds of oversimplifications become more adaptive in larger social networks, where people rarely encounter social information about their partners. Once people begin communicating through these concepts, they become entrenched in language, where they guide explicit beliefs about how people behave. These dynamics have become a significant focus of our lab’s ongoing work.

Persistent Diversity in a Globalized World

In addition to becoming larger and more anonymous, social networks have also become increasingly connected. How has this interconnection rewired cultural variation? One prediction from classic modernization theories was a mass cultural convergence, in which all countries adopted the values, technologies, and political systems of wealthy Western nations. The reality has been more complicated. Whereas some technologies and demographic trends have converged, values and political preferences have remained distinct across regions, and even diverged in some cases. One major theme of our lab’s work is seeking to understand the persistence of cultural diversity in a globalized world using formal models of cultural evolution.

Algorithm-Mediated Cultural Evolution

In the 21st century, many social networks have shifted to online spaces. Billions of people now belong to online social networks where they debate politics, technology, and lifestyles. Over the last several years, our lab has co-developed a theory of “algorithm-mediated social learning” to explain why these online spaces often seem to be moralized, emotional, and divisive. Our theory emphasizes the role of algorithms, which selectively promote attention-capturing information on newsfeeds. This process distorts users’ perceptions of social norms, leading people to think that toxicity and outrage are more common than they actually are, and to feel pressured to contribute this content. This process converges with other distinct mechanisms, such as the drive to contribute unique content, to foster a drift toward negative and outrage-laden discourse over time. To address this trend, we have also developed field interventions, including one algorithm that reduced toxicity during the 2024 presidential election by promoting a more representative set of voices.

Another emerging topic in the lab concerns how we can best foster innovation in online social networks. In other words, how can we make online social networks places where people come up with new and promising ideas, as well as places where people treat each other with kindness and respect? We are currently doing research that combines network science with our theory of algorithm-mediated social learning to answer this question.
